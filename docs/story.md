Prospectivity models rank ground by how much its chemistry resembles the ground around known deposits, and the number reported is almost always a cross-validated AUC, a score between zero and one for how well the model ranks mineralised cells above the rest, where one half means chance. I wanted to know how much of that number is real when the cross-validation is random. Known deposits cluster in districts, stream-sediment chemistry is smooth over tens of kilometres, and random folds put cells from the same district on both sides of the split, so the model is credited for remembering where things are. I wanted to measure that credit on public data, with the protocol written down before I fitted anything, so the answer could not drift toward whichever block size looked most dramatic.

## Choosing Nevada and gold

I needed a region with thousands of known producers and a survey whose laboratories had measured the same elements, because a laboratory batch effect would otherwise sit underneath the spatial effect I was trying to isolate. The survey is NURE, the national reconnaissance of stream sediments run in the late nineteen-seventies, and the deposits come from MRDS, the USGS database of mineral sites with their commodities and development status.

I profiled two candidates from the whole-country archives. Arizona with copper had enough producers, but its samples came from four laboratories with inconsistent element suites, and about half of them were soils rather than stream sediments. Nevada's {{extract.nure_quads}} quadrangles were all analysed by two laboratories, nearly all of its samples are stream sediments, and MRDS lists {{labels.n_positive_sites}} sites with gold as a primary commodity and a status of producer or past producer. I chose Nevada and gold, and rejected a sub-region of Nevada, which would have lost the batch comparison I expected to need.

![Retained stream-sediment samples coloured by analytical method family, with MRDS gold producer sites overlaid.](reports/figures/fig1_coverage.png)

The coverage map shows the complication the laboratory count had hidden. The samples split not into two laboratories but into three analytical method families, groups analysed by the same method and reporting the same elements, because one laboratory's samples went either to its own neutron-activation methods or to Oak Ridge methods. Each family occupies its own set of quadrangles; only {{features.n_cells_mixed_family}} cells hold samples from more than one family. The method family became the batch variable, and the map told me at the outset that holding out a family would also mean holding out a region.

## Finding out which elements could be used at all

The next question was which elements were measured consistently across all three families. A model that sees an element in two families and nothing in the third does not learn geochemistry; it learns the family. So I fixed a rule before any modelling and wrote it into the pre-registration. Keep an element if it is non-null in at least {{prep.suite_rule_min_nonnull_pct:.0f}} percent of records and censored in at most {{prep.suite_rule_max_censored_pct:.0f}} percent of its non-null records in every family, where a censored value is one reported only as below the detection limit.

![Share of retained samples below the detection limit for each suite element, by analytical method family, with the suite rule's ceiling marked.](reports/figures/fig2_censoring.png)

Exactly {{prep.n_suite_elements}} elements pass, namely {{prep.suite_elements}}. What the rule excluded matters more than what it kept. Arsenic, antimony, mercury and tungsten, the classic pathfinder elements for gold in Nevada (elements that are not the target but tend to occur with it), were absent or heavily censored in at least one family. The ten survivors are rock-forming elements and the light rare earths. I knew then that whatever the model learned would be about rock type rather than ore, and I wrote that limitation down before fitting anything.

Censoring in the retained suite is modest, {{prep.censored_pct_overall:.1f}} percent of values across the ten elements, with the worst case titanium in the LL family at {{prep.censored_pct_max:.1f}} percent. I substituted {{features.substitution_factor}} times the detection limit before the log-ratio transform, a labelled substitution rather than an estimate, and recorded the censored share for every element and family.

## Deciding what one row means

The samples are not independent observations, since hundreds share exact coordinates and replicate records exist, so a split at the sample level would test the model against copies of its own training data. I made the unit of analysis a 5 km grid cell in UTM zone 11N. A cell's features are the mean of its samples' centred log-ratio vectors, where the centred log-ratio divides each element by the geometric mean of all ten before taking logarithms, which removes the closure of compositional data. After the pre-registered exclusions {{prep.n_samples}} samples remain in {{features.n_cells}} cells; the median cell holds {{features.samples_per_cell_median:.0f}} samples and {{features.pct_cells_single_sample:.0f}} percent hold only one, so cell features are noisy.

A cell is positive if it contains at least one MRDS producer or past producer with gold as a primary commodity. A cell with no such site but with any site that mentions gold at any rank or status, a prospect, an occurrence, or a copper mine with by-product gold, is ambiguous, and I excluded it from the confirmatory population; {{labels.n_cells_ambiguous}} cells went that way. Every other sampled cell is a negative. The negatives are unlabelled, not barren. Nobody has certified that those cells hold no gold; they are cells where nobody recorded a producer, so this is positive-unlabelled learning and every precision and calibration figure is a lower bound.

The population is {{labels.n_cells}} cells with {{labels.n_positive_cells}} positives, a base rate of {{cv.base_rate_pct:.1f}} percent. The deposit-to-cell join is a per-cell count merged one-to-one and checked, because MRDS carries duplicates; its {{labels.n_positive_sites}} positive sites sit at {{labels.n_positive_sites_unique_coords}} distinct coordinates. Only {{labels.pct_positive_sites_in_sampled_cells:.0f}} percent of the positive sites fall in a cell with a retained sample; the rest cannot be scored and are outside the population.

## Writing the decision rule and the block size before fitting

Before I computed any result I wrote the pre-registration and froze it with the harness, so that a changed hash would fail verification. It fixed the question, the unit, the labels, the suite rule, two models with their tuning grids, the cross-validation schemes and the decision rule. The models are an L2 logistic regression on isometric log-ratio coordinates and gradient boosted trees, many small decision trees each fitted to the errors of the last, each tuned by an inner {{cv.k_inner}}-fold split under the same grouping as the outer split. The schemes are random stratified 5-fold with {{cv.n_repetitions}} repetitions; square spatial blocks of {{moran.block_sizes_km}} km with a random offset and allocation, {{cv.n_repetitions}} repetitions per size; leave-one-method-family-out; and leave-one-quadrangle-out.

The rule compares the boosted model's mean random-fold AUC with its mean block-fold AUC at a reference block size. Leakage is substantial if the difference is at least 0.05 and the min-max ranges across repetitions do not overlap. The model has skill under spatial cross-validation only if the block-fold mean is at least 0.60 and the minimum over repetitions is above 0.55; otherwise the frozen label is "{{headline.hgb.skill_label}}". That label is an operational threshold for useful skill, not a statistical test that AUC equals 0.5.

The reference block size could not be a number I liked the look of, so I pre-registered how it would be chosen. Moran's I measures how much nearby locations resemble each other compared with locations chosen at random. I would compute it for the cell label in 5 km distance bins on cell centroids, before any model was fitted, and take the smallest block size in the grid at or above the distance where it first falls below {{moran.threshold}}.

![Moran's I of the gold-producer label against distance between cell centroids, with the threshold and the resulting reference block size marked; the residual curve was added afterwards as an exploratory check.](reports/figures/fig3_moran.png)

The label's Moran's I is {{moran.i_bin1:.2f}} for cell pairs within {{moran.i_bin1_hi_km:.0f}} km, {{moran.i_bin2:.2f}} in the next bin, and first falls below the threshold at {{moran.range_km:.0f}} km. The reference block size is therefore {{moran.reference_block_km:.0f}} km, fixed by the rule before any AUC existed. Gold districts in Nevada are tens of kilometres across, and a block has to be larger than a district before a split stops sharing one between training and test.

## The random-fold result and why I did not trust it

This is the number a conventional pipeline would report. Under random stratified 5-fold cross-validation the boosted model reaches an AUC of {{cv.hgb.random.auc.mean:.3f}}, range {{cv.hgb.random.auc.min:.3f}} to {{cv.hgb.random.auc.max:.3f}} over {{cv.n_repetitions}} repetitions, and the logistic regression {{cv.lr.random.auc.mean:.3f}}. The boosted model's top ten percent of cells capture {{cv.hgb.random.capture_10.mean:.0%}} of held-out producer cells, a lift of {{headline.hgb.capture_10_lift_random:.2f}} over chance.

I did not trust it, and the correlogram was the reason. With Moran's I at {{moran.i_bin1:.2f}} in the first distance bin, a random fold leaves nearly every held-out cell with training neighbours that share its district and its lithology. The random-fold score measures how well the model fills gaps between sampled cells it has seen, which is not the question an exploration geologist asks of new ground.

## Holding out a method family

The second pre-registered check asks a harsher question. Leave-one-group-out cross-validation holds out an entire analytical method family at a time, so the model scores samples from a method it never saw; if it has learned chemistry it should transfer, and if it has learned which family did the assay it should not. The pooled AUC over the three held-out families is {{batch.hgb.lofo.auc:.3f}} for the boosted model, below chance, and {{batch.hgb.lofo_minus_blockref:.3f}} below the block-fold value at the reference size. Within each held-out family the ranking is slightly better than chance, {{batch.hgb.lofo.auc_LL:.3f}}, {{batch.hgb.lofo.auc_SR:.3f}} and {{batch.hgb.lofo.auc_OR:.3f}}; the pooled value is lower because the families' score distributions are offset from one another. The frozen batch-trap rule is met for both models.

I had expected a batch effect, since the pre-registration named it as the second trap. The covariate model that probes it is exploratory. Adding three one-hot family indicators raises the boosted model's random-fold AUC by {{explore.labcov.hgb.random_gain:.3f}} to {{explore.labcov.hgb.random.auc.mean:.3f}}, and at the reference block size the covariate model scores {{explore.labcov.hgb.blockref.auc.mean:.3f}}. The family is a cheap proxy, since each covers a contiguous set of quadrangles and each has its own base rate, {{labels.base_rate_pct_LL:.1f}} percent of cells positive for LL against {{labels.base_rate_pct_SR:.1f}} for SR and {{labels.base_rate_pct_OR:.1f}} for OR.

What I can claim is narrower than "the model learned the lab". Holding out method families produces distribution shift associated with both analytical family and region, and the two are confounded, because the families occupy different quadrangles, differ in sieve fraction and sample different geology. The family indicators are learnable proxies for both method and region; this dataset cannot isolate a causal laboratory effect. It does show that an analyst who one-hot-encodes the laboratory as a nuisance covariate and validates with random folds would report a better model that generalises worse.

## Which elements carry the signal, and why that is lithology

This part is exploratory; the pre-registration lists the element interpretation as such, and I did it after seeing the primary result. Relative to the other cells, producer cells are enriched in {{explore.contrast_most_enriched}} and depleted in {{explore.contrast_most_depleted}}, and the logistic coefficients mapped back to centred-log-ratio space put {{explore.lr_coef_max_element}} most positive and {{explore.lr_coef_min_element}} most negative. Held-out permutation importance for the boosted model is small for every element; the leading one, among {{explore.top_elements_hgb}}, is worth {{explore.top_element_hgb_importance:.3f}} AUC.

More scandium, vanadium and iron with less lanthanum, cerium and thorium is a contrast between mafic-to-intermediate and felsic rock, not a hydrothermal signature. It is consistent with many Nevada producers sitting in andesitic volcanic terrain, and it says nothing about the ore itself. None of the ten elements is a pathfinder; the pathfinders were excluded by the rule fixed before fitting. Lithology is spatially smooth, which is why random folds overstate a model built on it. Moran's I of the random-fold residuals is {{explore.moran_residual_bin1:.2f}} in the first bin against {{moran.i_bin1:.2f}} for the label, so the features explain little of the label's spatial structure.

## An exploratory check with distance buffers

This part is exploratory. I decided to do it after seeing the primary result, and I report it as such; the frozen metrics and the decision rule are unchanged by it. Square blocks touch at their edges, so block cross-validation guarantees that a held-out cell's block is unseen but not that any distance separates it from the nearest training cell. Buffered cross-validation adds that guarantee by removing training cells within a chosen radius of every held-out cell, and I buffered the inner tuning split as well.

I ran a matched suite on the baseline {{moran.reference_block_km:.0f}} km blocks and both model grids, with radii of 0, 5, 10 and 25 km and three repetitions, fewer than the baseline, so the comparison belongs inside this suite. At 0 km the boosted model's mean AUC is {{buffered.hgb.buffer0.auc_mean:.3f}}, range {{buffered.hgb.buffer0.auc_min:.3f}} to {{buffered.hgb.buffer0.auc_max:.3f}}. At 25 km it is {{buffered.hgb.buffer25.auc_mean:.3f}}, range {{buffered.hgb.buffer25.auc_min:.3f}} to {{buffered.hgb.buffer25.auc_max:.3f}}, with a mean of {{buffered.hgb.buffer25.removed_mean:.0f}} training cells excluded per outer fold; the two intermediate radii fall between. The decline is small, but a buffer removes training support as well as neighbours, so the difference cannot be attributed to leakage alone. The check neither rescues the model nor changes the verdict.

## What I concluded

Most of this model's apparent skill is spatial leakage. By the rule I froze before fitting, the leakage is substantial, and at the pre-registered block size the model does not meet the useful-skill threshold, with the logistic regression behaving the same way. What signal survives tracks a mafic-versus-felsic lithology contrast, and holding out an analytical method family produces a distribution shift in which family and region cannot be separated.

## What I would not claim

I would not claim that the block-fold AUC equals chance; the frozen label is an operational threshold, not a hypothesis test, and the ranking is slightly better than random at every block size. I would not make an exploration recommendation, because the signal is lithological rather than a pathfinder signature, the probabilities sit in a narrow band around the base rate, the negatives are unlabelled, and a third of known producers lie outside the sampled cells. I would not claim to have isolated a laboratory effect, since family and region are confounded here, and I would not claim anything about gold prospectivity outside the three method families and the ten-element suite.

## What I would do next with another week

I would take the protocol to a survey in which the pathfinder elements were measured consistently, to compare this lithological baseline against data that might carry a mineralisation signal. The few quadrangles where two families overlap are the only place in these data where the family-and-region confound might be loosened, and I would start there. And I would turn this project's synthetic leakage test into a harness sensor that checks the distance from every held-out unit to its nearest training unit, so that the random-fold number cannot be reported by accident.
